## The numbers

Take a silicon BJT with the following values.

- Supply: $V_{CC} = 12\,\text{V}$
- Divider: $R_1 = 68\,\text{k}\Omega$ and $R_2 = 12\,\text{k}\Omega$
- Collector resistor: $R_C = 4.7\,\text{k}\Omega$
- Emitter resistor: $R_E = 1.1\,\text{k}\Omega$
- Base-emitter junction: $V_{BE} = 0.7\,\text{V}$

Three assumptions keep the arithmetic simple.

1. The divider current is much larger than the base current, so base loading is ignored.
2. The collector and emitter currents are equal, so $I_C = I_E$.
3. The transistor stays in its active region, so it can amplify.

## Four steps

Step 1, the base voltage. The divider scales the supply down.

$$
V_B = V_{CC}\,\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}
$$

The divider holds the base at 1.8 V.

Step 2, the emitter voltage. The base-emitter junction drops about 0.7 V when it conducts.

$$
V_E = V_B - V_{BE}
$$

The emitter sits 1.1 V above ground.

Step 3, the bias current. Ohm's law at the emitter gives the current directly.

$$
I_C = I_E = \frac{V_E}{R_E}
$$

With $R_E = 1.1\,\text{k}\Omega$, the collector current is 1.0 mA.

Step 4, the collector-to-emitter voltage. Walk the loop from supply to ground.

$$
V_{CE} = V_{CC} - I_C R_C - I_E R_E
$$

Plugging in the numbers gives a collector-to-emitter voltage of 6.2 V. That sits near half the supply, leaving symmetric room for the output to swing.

## Key takeaways

- Divider, junction drop, Ohm's law at the emitter, then the loop. Four steps, one bias point.
- A healthy Q point sits near half the supply.
