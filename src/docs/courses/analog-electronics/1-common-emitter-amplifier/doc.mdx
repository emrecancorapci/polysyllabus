## Why amplify with a transistor

A microphone produces a few millivolts. A loudspeaker wants volts. Something in between must make small signals large without wrecking their shape.

That something is usually a transistor. This lesson uses the bipolar junction transistor, or BJT. It has three terminals: the base, the collector and the emitter.

A small voltage between base and emitter controls a much larger current from collector to emitter. A transistor with a few surrounding parts makes one amplifier stage. Wired as a common-emitter stage, the transistor becomes the workhorse of analog electronics.

## Why the emitter is called common

The input signal is applied between the base and the emitter. The output signal is taken between the collector and the emitter. Both loops share the emitter node. That shared node gives the stage its name.

In practice the emitter is grounded for signals. So the input enters at the base and the output appears at the collector.

## The circuit and its bias

A transistor needs the right resting voltages before it can amplify anything. Setting those resting values is called biasing. The resting values together form the bias point, also called the Q point.

A transistor must also stay in its active region. That is the range where it amplifies rather than acting as a switch.

Around the transistor sit four resistors and three capacitors.

- $R_1$ runs from the positive supply $V_{CC}$ down to the base.
- $R_2$ runs from the base down to ground.
- $R_1$ and $R_2$ together form a voltage divider that fixes the base voltage.
- $R_C$ runs from the supply to the collector and converts current back into voltage.
- $R_E$ runs from the emitter to ground and stabilizes the bias current.
- $C_{in}$ and $C_{out}$ are coupling capacitors. They pass signal but block DC.
- $C_E$ is the bypass capacitor, placed across $R_E$.

A coupling capacitor lets signal variations through while blocking DC. That keeps this stage's bias from disturbing the source and the load.

The bypass capacitor does a different job. At signal frequencies it effectively shorts $R_E$. The emitter then becomes a signal ground. The DC bias current through $R_E$ is unchanged.

## Worked example: finding the DC bias point

Take a silicon BJT with the following values.

- Supply: $V_{CC} = 12\,\text{V}$
- Divider: $R_1 = 68\,\text{k}\Omega$ and $R_2 = 12\,\text{k}\Omega$
- Collector resistor: $R_C = 4.7\,\text{k}\Omega$
- Emitter resistor: $R_E = 1.1\,\text{k}\Omega$
- Base-emitter junction: $V_{BE} = 0.7\,\text{V}$

Three assumptions keep the arithmetic simple.

1. The divider current is much larger than the base current, so base loading is ignored.
2. The collector and emitter currents are equal, so $I_C = I_E$.
3. The transistor stays in its active region, so it can amplify.

Step 1, the base voltage. The divider scales the supply down.

$$
V_B = V_{CC}\,\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}
$$

The divider holds the base at 1.8 V.

Step 2, the emitter voltage. The base-emitter junction drops about 0.7 V when it conducts.

$$
V_E = V_B - V_{BE}
$$

The emitter sits 1.1 V above ground.

Step 3, the bias current. Ohm's law at the emitter gives the current directly.

$$
I_C = I_E = \frac{V_E}{R_E}
$$

With $R_E = 1.1\,\text{k}\Omega$, the collector current is 1.0 mA.

Step 4, the collector-to-emitter voltage. Walk the loop from supply to ground.

$$
V_{CE} = V_{CC} - I_C R_C - I_E R_E
$$

Plugging in the numbers gives a collector-to-emitter voltage of 6.2 V. That sits near half the supply, leaving symmetric room for the output to swing.

## The small-signal view

Now freeze the bias and ask how the circuit responds to small wiggles.

For signals the transistor simplifies to one number, its transconductance $g_m$. Transconductance says how much the collector current changes for each volt of change between base and emitter.

$$
g_m = \frac{I_C}{V_T}
$$

Here $V_T$ is the thermal voltage set by the physics of the junction. We assume $V_T = 25\,\text{mV}$, the usual room-temperature value.

At the bias point above, $I_C = 1.0\,\text{mA}$. The transconductance is 40 mS.

The siemens, symbol S, is the unit of conductance. One siemens is one ampere per volt.

For gain we also ignore the transistor's output resistance. The collector behaves as an ideal current source for signals.

## The gain with the emitter bypassed

With $C_E$ in place, the emitter is a signal ground. The whole input appears across the base-emitter junction, so $v_{in} = v_{be}$.

A positive wiggle at the base increases the collector current. The extra current through $R_C$ pulls the collector voltage down. So a positive input wiggle gives a negative output wiggle.

Writing the output as the drop across $R_C$ gives the gain.

$$
A_v = \frac{v_{out}}{v_{in}} = -g_m R_C
$$

With $g_m = 40\,\text{mS}$ and $R_C = 4.7\,\text{k}\Omega$, the gain is $A_v = -188$.

The minus sign is the inversion. It means the output is 180 degrees out of phase with the input.

## The gain with the emitter unbypassed

Remove $C_E$ and the emitter becomes unbypassed. The circuit now behaves differently. The input must push signal current through $R_E$ as well as through the junction.

Any rise in emitter current also raises the emitter voltage. That rise pushes back against the input, because only $v_{be}$ controls the current. This opposing effect is called negative feedback.

Working through the two voltage drops gives:

$$
A_v = \frac{-g_m R_C}{1 + g_m R_E}
$$

The feedback factor $1 + g_m R_E$ equals 45 here. So the gain becomes $A_v \approx -4.18$. That is a factor of 45 smaller than the bypassed gain.

An equivalent form uses the intrinsic emitter resistance $r_e = 1/g_m$. At our bias point $g_m$ is 40 mS, so $r_e$ is 25 ohm. The gain then reads $A_v = -R_C/(r_e + R_E)$. This form is easy to remember.

The lost gain buys stability. The gain now depends mainly on the two resistors. It depends only weakly on $g_m$, which drifts with temperature and current. Designers often split $R_E$ into two parts and bypass only one of them. The result lands between the two extremes.

## Common mistakes

Watch for these on exams.

- **Forgetting the junction drop.** The emitter is 0.7 V below the base, not at the base voltage.
- **Mixing up the divider legs.** The base voltage is the fraction across $R_2$, the lower leg.
- **Losing the minus sign.** The output moves opposite to the input, a 180 degree shift.
- **Trusting the divider too much.** If the resistors are too large, base current pulls the base voltage down.
- **Confusing the two grounds.** $C_E$ grounds the emitter for signals only. The bias current still flows through $R_E$.

## Key takeaways

- The emitter is the terminal shared by the input and output loops, which names the stage.
- A divider plus an emitter resistor sets a bias point that barely depends on which transistor you solder in.
- With the emitter bypassed the gain is $A_v = -g_m R_C$, large and inverted.
- With $R_E$ unbypassed the gain is $A_v = -g_m R_C/(1 + g_m R_E)$, smaller but steadier.
